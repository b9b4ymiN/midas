# Growth sources — what each requires as evidence

A source you cannot evidence is a source you cannot project.

## Volume

**Evidence:** units, tonnes, subscribers, transactions, room-nights — a physical
or countable measure, disclosed by the company.

The best kind of growth: it compounds without needing new capital, new markets or
new acquisitions. If a company reports volume growth alongside stable price, the
business is working.

**Watch:** volume growth that requires ever-rising marketing spend is buying
volume, not earning it. Check volume growth against the sales-and-marketing line.

## Price

**Evidence:** realised price per unit, or price/mix disclosure.

Conditional by nature. Price growth **with volume holding** is pricing power and
belongs in a terminal assumption. Price growth **with volume falling** is cost
pass-through, and projecting it forward assumes customers keep absorbing
increases while already leaving.

**Watch:** mix improvement reported as price. Selling more of the expensive
product is real, but it is finite in a way a price increase is not — you run out
of customers to trade up.

## Expansion

**Evidence:** store, plant or capacity count, and ideally returns on the new units.

Finite by construction: it works until the addressable market is covered. The
question is not whether new units add revenue — they do — but whether they earn
what the existing ones earn.

**Watch:** new-unit ROIC below the corporate average means the expansion is
diluting returns while flattering revenue. Also watch cannibalisation: a new
store that takes an existing store's customers adds less than it appears to.

## Acquisition

**Evidence:** acquired revenue, disclosed separately, plus the price paid.

Not performance until you know the price. The acquired revenue consolidates and
flatters growth immediately; the cost of buying it sits on the balance sheet as
goodwill and in the share count if it was funded with equity.

**Watch:** this is the same signal as the capital-allocation metrics in the
financial dashboard. If acquisitions supply most of the growth, read the goodwill
trend and the diluted share count before calling any of it growth.

## Currency

**Evidence:** the company's own constant-currency disclosure, when it gives one.

Not earned. Quote the organic figure. A currency tailwind reverses, and a model
that projects it forward is projecting an exchange rate, which is a different and
much harder claim than the one being made.

## When the company does not split it

Many do not. Then say so — "volume/price split not disclosed" — rather than
inferring one from a ratio. An inferred split becomes a fact two documents later,
and the person acting on it will not know it was inferred.
