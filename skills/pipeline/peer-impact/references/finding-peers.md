# Finding the candidates

Layer 1: cast the net worldwide by revenue mix. Layer 2 (`impact-channels.md`)
does the filtering.

## Take the country filter off

The standard peer search is *same sector, same market*, and for a multiples table
that is correct — a Thai company trades against Thai comparables because it faces
the same rates, the same currency and the same investor base.

For impact it is exactly wrong. Competition for a scarce input is global by
construction. Everyone canning tuna is bidding for fish from the same oceans
whether they are in Bangkok, Busan or San Diego. A search restricted to the home
market returns companies that sell into the same domestic supermarkets while
missing every firm that competes for the actual input.

Run the search on **what the company sells and what it buys**. Geography enters
only as context — regulation, cost base, currency — when you interpret the
result, not when you gather it.

## Start from the mix

Segment shares come from the **filing** — the annual report, the 56-1, or the
quarterly results release. If they arrived through the data layer they carry the
provider's own labelling and uneven coverage, so cross-check before a whole
competitive analysis rests on them.

Search per segment, weighted by share. A segment at 47% of revenue deserves a
real search; one at 7% does not define a competitor set at all.

## Where to look

| Route | What it gives |
|---|---|
| The filing's own competitive section | Companies management names. Start here — it is free and it is their own view of who they fight. |
| Trade associations for the product | Membership lists are close to a complete producer roster in commodity industries. |
| Import/export and customs data | Who ships the same product to the same destination markets. |
| Trade press | Capacity announcements, price commentary, who is bidding for what. |
| Customer disclosures | A large retailer's supplier list names your shelf competitors directly. |
| Global sector screens | Useful only after the mix is known; screening by sector alone reproduces the domestic-only failure. |

## Vertical neighbours count too

A supplier or customer can move your margin more than a competitor. Someone who
buys the same input you do — even for an entirely different end product — is a
supply-channel competitor. Pet-food manufacturers and canned-tuna producers do
not compete for a single customer and can compete hard for the same fish.

Ask **who else bids for the thing I buy**, not just who else sells the thing I
sell.

## When the mix is unusual

A conglomerate whose segments share nothing has no single peer set. Build one
per segment above ~15% of revenue and say so, rather than forcing a single list
that fits none of them.

## What "enough" looks like

Three to six candidates that survive Layer 2 is a working set. More than that
and the list stops being monitorable; monitored by nobody is the same as absent.

The count that matters more is how many you **considered and dropped** — that
number is what tells a reader whether the search was wide. Record it.
